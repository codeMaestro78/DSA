# Behavioral interview

## What you are preparing

Prepare accurate accounts of how you acted, made decisions, worked with others and learned. Technical sophistication does not replace an answer to the actual question. Conversely, a polished story does not replace truthful evidence.

[OFFICIAL] Microsoft's guidance recommends STAR(R), including reflection. [Interview tips](https://careers.microsoft.com/v2/global/en/hiring-tips/interview-tips). The templates, suggested story count and scoring in this knowledge base are original practice tools.

## Build a usable story inventory

Start with six real episodes: ownership, disagreement, failure, ambiguity, prioritization and user/customer impact. An episode can support several themes if the relevant facts remain the same. Academic/team projects and volunteering can provide examples; identify the setting rather than presenting them as employment.

For each, write the situation briefly, your responsibility, the alternatives considered, your actions, the observed outcome and what you changed later. Distinguish “I implemented the retry policy” from “the team delivered the service.” If you do not know an outcome metric, use a verifiable qualitative result and state the gap.

## Make decisions visible

Weak: “We collaborated and finished on time.” Stronger structure: “The deadline was [real constraint]. I proposed [bounded scope] because [tradeoff]. I asked [stakeholder] to confirm [acceptance condition]. The result was [actual outcome]. Next time I would [specific adjustment].” This is an adaptable structure, not an experience to claim.

## Handle followups

Expect “What did you personally do?”, “Why not the alternative?”, “What evidence changed your mind?”, and “What would your teammate say?” Keep enough factual detail to answer these without changing the story. Do not invent dialogue or certainty about another person's intentions.

If you have no direct example, say so and offer the closest honest example. For a hypothetical scenario, label your answer as what you would do rather than what you did.

## Practice

Record a two-minute real failure story. Ask a reviewer to identify your decision, consequence and learning. If those are unclear, revise the structure before adding more vocabulary.

<details><summary>Self-check</summary>

A useful answer has a specific context, clear ownership, reasoned action, supported outcome and credible reflection. It avoids blaming others, casting yourself as flawless or manufacturing a success ending for a failure question.

</details>

## Checklist

- [ ] All personal facts are true and shareable.
- [ ] My contribution and the team's result are separate.
- [ ] I can explain a mistake without avoiding responsibility.
